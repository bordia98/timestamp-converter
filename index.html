The Complete Guide to Unix Epoch & Timestamps

A Unix timestamp (also known as Epoch time, POSIX time, or UNIX time) represents the total number of elapsed seconds since the Unix Epoch: 00:00:00 UTC on Thursday, 1 January 1970 (excluding leap seconds). It serves as the universal standard for measuring time across distributed database systems, operating systems, networking protocols, and web servers.

Why January 1, 1970?

When the initial versions of Unix were being developed at Bell Labs in the early 1970s, engineers needed an arbitrary, recent, and round reference point in time. They chose midnight UTC on January 1, 1970. Because computers measure time by incrementing an integer counter on system clock ticks, Unix time allows algorithms to perform instantaneous date arithmetic (subtraction, comparison, and sorting) without having to account for irregular calendar complexities like time zones, month lengths, or daylight saving time until final presentation.

The Year 2038 Problem (Y2038 / Epochalypse)

In traditional 32-bit computing architectures, Unix timestamps are stored as a 32-bit signed integer. The maximum value a 32-bit signed integer can hold is 2,147,483,647. At exactly 03:14:07 UTC on Tuesday, 19 January 2038, this counter will overflow and wrap into negative numbers (interpreting the date as December 13, 1901).

Modern operating systems and 64-bit systems use 64-bit integers for timestamp storage, pushing the next overflow boundary approximately 292 billion years into the future.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

Does Unix Epoch include leap seconds?

No. Standard POSIX time repeats the timestamp value of the 59th second during a leap second, intentionally keeping every day equal to exactly 86,400 seconds to simplify calendar math across computer networks.

How does auto-detection distinguish seconds from milliseconds?

Current Unix timestamps in seconds are 10 digits (e.g. 1773446400). Millisecond timestamps are 13 digits (e.g. 1773446400000). Microseconds are 16 digits, and nanoseconds are 19 digits. Our converter inspects the magnitude of the number and chooses the exact scaling factor automatically.

Is any timestamp data saved on your server?

Zero data is saved. This application runs entirely inside your browser using the client-side JavaScript Intl.DateTimeFormat API. No server logs or cookies are generated.
